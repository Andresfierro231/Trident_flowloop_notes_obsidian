The Texas A&M University (TAMU) natural circulation loop, operated by Reis, Seo,
and Hassan [9], represents the state of the art for such validation data. The experiment used
particle image velocimetry (PIV) to measure two-dimensional velocity fields midway up the
riser leg of the loop and recorded steady-state thermocouple readings around the loop, for
two working fluids: water as a baseline, and HITEC, a eutectic molten salt mixture. These
experiments were conducted in identical loop geometries at four constant heater powers each
until thermal equilibrium was achieved. This two-fluid approach allows us to isolate the
effects of the fluid properties from those of the loop geometry, making the TAMU dataset an
attractive validation target, especially for demonstrating the unique difficulties and different
approaches needed for properly modeling the natural circulation of molten salt.
The need for validated models increases further when the development of new, digital-
twin technology for advanced nuclear systems is considered; particularly relevant for this
thesis are those in development at the University of Texas at Austin.
Digital twins are
anticipated to improve both the efficiency and safety of operating nuclear systems [10], but
the reduced-order models and neural networks used for these applications must, at their
core, be trained and evaluated against data with known quality.
A rigorously validated
CFD model can serve as both a reference solution and synthetic training data generator
for quantities and locations that physical instrumentation cannot reach or did not measure.
That intention increases the pressure for present work, as models developed downstream
from this work inherit the quality of the data produced here, whose credibility must be
quantified.
