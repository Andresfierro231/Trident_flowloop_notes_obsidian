One of the characteristics that contribute to the passive safety of some MSR designs is
natural circulation. Natural circulation is the transport of fluids resultant from the buoy-
ant forces that arise from the thermal expansion and contraction of a fluid from heating
and cooling without any external pumping required. Since the driving mechanism of this
transport is from underlying physics, natural circulation can continue to remove heat from
a reactor core even in total absence of electrical power or operator action, making it an
attractive choice for emergency (and sometime normal) heat transport in advanced reactor
designs [4]. Designing around these physics make the ability to make accurate predictions
of flow rates, temperature distributions, and stability behavior an essential part of licensing
and safe operation [5]. This requirement for accurate predictions necessitates accurate data,
which is sparsely available for designs relying on working fluids other than water, such as
MSRs.
Acquiring that predictive ability experimentally is difficult. Performing natural circula-
tion experiments in reactor-like conditions are expensive and tricky. Molten salts are often
corrosive to materials that are not specialized to deal with them. This difficulty compounds
with the high temperatures needed to melt reactor salts like FLiBe, further complicating
instrumentation for acquiring experimental data. Further still, the thermophysical property
data necessary to interpret such experiments often carries appreciable uncertainty or is not
agreed upon in the associated literature [6, 7]. Consequently, high-quality validation data
for molten salt natural circulation is scarce, leading to a reliance on modeling and simula-
tion for reactor design and licensing. Models and simulations themselves can only be relied
upon once they are systematically validated against existing experimental data. Beyond
that, development of these models is a challenge in and of itself; complications such as the
tight coupling of buoyancy-driven flow with its own temperature field and the fluid’s own
