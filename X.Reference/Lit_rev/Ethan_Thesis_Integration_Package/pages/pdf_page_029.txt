CFD Simulation of Natural Circulation Loops
Several groups have applied OpenFOAM to natural circulation simulation.
Luzzi et al.
[29] validated both a one-dimensional, semi-analytical model and a CFD model made using
OpenFOAM V4 against a rectangular loop that heated its bottom leg and cooled its top
leg.
It was found in both the experiments and the corresponding simulations that not
all configurations exhibited stable behavior.
This observation of occasional instability is
not an uncommon one for symmetric rectangular and circular geometries.
As unstable
configurations tend to be oscillatory, Pini et al. [30] exploited OpenFOAM’s open-source
nature to couple simulation data directly to MATLAB for eigenvalue-based prediction of
oscillatory behavior in rectangular loops.
Battistini et al.
[31] extended the toolset to
Large Eddy Simulation (LES) of the DYNASTY facility, predicting flow reversals and re-
laminarization, phenomena typical of unstable natural circulation configurations, that had
been absent from previous Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) models of the facility.
Reviewing these OpenFOAM studies, readers can begin to see how consequential the
choice of how to handle turbulence modeling can be. For modeling natural circulation sys-
tems that are already sensitive to material property inputs, adding the historically tricky
problem of turbulence modeling complicates matters. Nalbandyan et al. [32] demonstrated
this directly, testing three different turbulence models (namely the k −ωSST, k −ωSSTLM,
and realizable k −ϵ models) applied to a 3D CFD against their 1-D model of the DY-
NASTY loop filled with water. Depending on the turbulence model, their relative errors
versus the experiment for mass flow rate varied by 7.2% −16.2% and their temperature
difference through the cooler section of their loop was off by 1.6K −2.3K. For molten salt
loops specifically, Simamora and Lee [33] used ANSYS Fluent to compare laminar and tran-
sitional (k-ω SSTLM) treatments of a two-dimensional nitrate salt loop model. The laminar
model underpredicted the circulation velocity by roughly 30%, the transitional model by
only 4.2%. The authors attribute the improvement largely to the improved mixing resultant
