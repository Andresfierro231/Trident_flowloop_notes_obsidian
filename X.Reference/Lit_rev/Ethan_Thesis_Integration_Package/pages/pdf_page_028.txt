The facility most relevant to the present work is the TAMU natural circulation loop
operated by Reis, Seo, and Hassan [9].
It occupies a unique place in the literature: it
studied water and HITEC salt within an identical geometry, and its transparent quartz
test section allows the use of particle image velocimetry (PIV), providing radially resolved
velocity profiles without perturbing the flow the way a physical flow meter would. Companion
publications document argon injection experiments [23], the consequences of an inadvertent
salt solidification event [24], and the dataset has since served as a validation target for the
SAM system code at high Prandtl number conducted by the experimentalists themselves
[25]. The facility, its instrumentation, and its test matrix are described in detail in Chapter
4.
Interpreting any of these experiments, and especially attempting to simulate them, rests
on the fidelity of the working fluid’s thermophysical properties. Beyond the density variation
that generates the buoyant force itself important properties such as the specific heat deter-
mines the temperatures at which a given heating and cooling configuration equilibrates, and
viscosity governs how readily the fluid circulates. Comprehensive single-source property col-
lections for molten salts are rare. Sohal et al. [7] compiled an engineering database of liquid
salt properties at Idaho National Laboratory, but its entries for HITEC (NaNO3-NaNO2-
KNO3, approximately 7-49-44 by mole and 7-40-53 by weight) trace back to the compilation
of Janz and Tomkins [26] and, for viscosity and specific heat, have been superseded by more
recent measurements. The review supporting this claim and the modern property suite se-
lected for this work, are presented in Chapter 4 and Appendix B. For water, its properties
are well-characterized by the IAPWS-IF97 with marginal uncertainties for temperatures in
the range of 273.15 K - 1073.15 K and pressures up to 100 MPa [27, 28].
The polyno-
mial fits used in this work reproduce reference values within 0.50 % over the experimental
temperature range (Appendix B).
