Abstract

Natural circulation loops are integral to passive safety systems in advanced nuclear reactor
designs, yet their computational modeling remains challenging due to the strong coupling
between buoyancy-driven flow, conjugate heat transfer, and temperature-dependent fluid
properties. This thesis works to demonstrate a comprehensive Verification and Validation
study of a three-dimensional compuational fluid dynamics simulation of the single-phase nat-
ural convection experimental facility operated by Texas A&M University and validate this
model against the experimental data of Reis, Seo, and Hassan (2024). The facility used two
fluids, water and HITEC molten salt, a eutectic nitrate salt mix chosen for its low melting
point and similar behavior to molten salts intended for use in molten salt reactors. For both
working fluids, four experiments were conducted each, varying the loop’s primary heater
power between each case and studying the effects that change had on the velocity profiles
within the system. Simulations of the system were performed in OpenFOAM v13 using
a structured, fully-conformal, purely-hexahedral mesh generated with the classy_blocks
library python library. A segmented first-order model was first built to reduce energy ac-
counting uncertainties associated with the experiment, and better account for passive heat
losses throughout the system.

Chapter 1

Introduction


1.1      Motivation

The global demand for electricity is large and ever-increasing. As Earth’s population contin-
ues to grow, countries develop, and living standards rise; technology advances as well, com-
pounding on the expanding demand for power with electric cars and the explosive growth
of energy-intensive data centers for greater and greater artificial intelligence workloads. To
sustain this growth, it is imperative that we prioritize the capacity of our power infrastruc-
ture [1]. That increased demand cannot be met by simply continuing to expand fossil-fuel
facilities; climate change demands that new capacity, and eventually the existing fossil-fuel
fleet, be filled by low-carbon sources [2].
   Nuclear power is one of few technologies that can satisfy both the capacity and climate
demands facing the near future. Nuclear’s ability to supply large amounts of reliable, carbon-
free energy independent of factors that may inhibit other carbon-free technologies such as
weather and geography. Much of the renewed interest in nuclear power is centered on new,
Generation IV reactor concepts. Among theses concepts are Molten Salt Reactors (MSRs),
that promise higher outlet temperature, leading to greater thermal efficiency, while being
operable a low pressures and having passive safety characteristics that their light-water

========== PDF PAGE 18 ==========

predecessors cannot match [3].
   One of the characteristics that contribute to the passive safety of some MSR designs is
natural circulation. Natural circulation is the transport of fluids resultant from the buoy-
ant forces that arise from the thermal expansion and contraction of a fluid from heating
and cooling without any external pumping required. Since the driving mechanism of this
transport is from underlying physics, natural circulation can continue to remove heat from
a reactor core even in total absence of electrical power or operator action, making it an
attractive choice for emergency (and sometime normal) heat transport in advanced reactor
designs [4]. Designing around these physics make the ability to make accurate predictions
of flow rates, temperature distributions, and stability behavior an essential part of licensing
and safe operation [5]. This requirement for accurate predictions necessitates accurate data,
which is sparsely available for designs relying on working fluids other than water, such as
MSRs.
   Acquiring that predictive ability experimentally is difficult. Performing natural circula-
tion experiments in reactor-like conditions are expensive and tricky. Molten salts are often
corrosive to materials that are not specialized to deal with them. This difficulty compounds
with the high temperatures needed to melt reactor salts like FLiBe, further complicating
instrumentation for acquiring experimental data. Further still, the thermophysical property
data necessary to interpret such experiments often carries appreciable uncertainty or is not
agreed upon in the associated literature [6, 7]. Consequently, high-quality validation data
for molten salt natural circulation is scarce, leading to a reliance on modeling and simula-
tion for reactor design and licensing. Models and simulations themselves can only be relied
upon once they are systematically validated against existing experimental data. Beyond
that, development of these models is a challenge in and of itself; complications such as the
tight coupling of buoyancy-driven flow with its own temperature field and the fluid’s own
temperature dependent properties, conjugate heat transfer through solid domains such as
pipe walls and external insulation, as well as even subtle geometric details of the facility’s

assembly [8].
   The Texas A&M University (TAMU) natural circulation loop, operated by Reis, Seo,
and Hassan [9], represents the state of the art for such validation data. The experiment used

   The need for validated models increases further when the development of new, digital-
twin technology for advanced nuclear systems is considered; particularly relevant for this

for quantities and locations that physical instrumentation cannot reach or did not measure.
That intention increases the pressure for present work, as models developed downstream

1.2      Objectives

The objectives of this thesis are:

  1. To develop a complete, automated CFD modeling pipeline for the TAMU natural
      circulation loop using OpenFOAM V13, that accepts geometry, physics, and study
      configuration files for a given experiment or suite of cases and automatically generates
      a fully conformal, fully hexahedral mesh, applies proper initial and boundary conditions
      from those configuration files, pre-process to ensure desired data collection is properly
      mapped to the mesh and captured appropriately, execute the simulation to convergence,

      ary condition.

2.1     Natural Circulation Fundamentals

Before examining what’s required to model and simulate natural circulation, the quirks of
molten salt, or how to best determine if a simulated result is even correct, it is important to
first understand the fundamentals of natural circulation.
   Natural circulation is the transport of a fluid resulting from heating and cooling that fluid
at different locations within a closed system. Heating causes the fluid to expand, lowering its

                                              10


========== PDF PAGE 25 ==========

local density, whereas cooling causes it to contract, raising its local density. The consequence
of those density changes can be seen by considering a fluid parcel of volume V and density
ρ surrounded by fluid of density ρ0 . By Archimedes’ principle, the surrounding fluid exerts
an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the displaced fluid,


                                            Fb = ρ0 g V,                                   (2.1)


where g is the gravitational acceleration, while the parcel itself weighs ρgV , leaving a net
vertical force
                                        Fnet = (ρ0 − ρ) g V.                               (2.2)

In a loop that heats its fluid at a low elevation and cools it at a higher one, this imbalance
can drive a continuous, self-sustained flow that requires no external pumping power, which
is what we called natural circulation [13]. Since the density of a fluid is a function of its
temperature, engineering analyses over modest temperature ranges often linearize the density
about a reference state (ρ0 , T0 ) as


                                ρ(T ) ≈ ρ0 + (∂ρ/∂T )p (T − T0 ),                          (2.3)


which is conventionally rewritten in terms of the volumetric thermal expansion coefficient,

                                   ρ(T ) ≈ ρ0 [ 1 − β (T − T0 ) ] .                        (2.4)

proximation was formalized by Spiegel and Veronis [14] and later tightened by Gray and
Giorgini [15]. The approximation treat density as constant in the unsteady and convective
terms and its linear variation is kept only in the gravitational body force while each other
physical property is held constant. Gray and Giorgini quantified when that treatment is
appropriate only when properties vary from their reference value by less than 10%, which for

2.2     Molten Salt Natural Circulation Experiments

Experimental data for molten salt natural circulation is limited by the practical challenges
of high-temperature operation, corrosion, and optical access, though efforts toward charac-
terizing the thermal-hydraulic behavior of salt loops span more than a decade. Srivastava
et al. [21] performed one of the first combined experiment/code validation exercises on a
molten nitrate salt loop, studying the transient evolution and subsequent steady state of
the loop’s flow and temperature distributions. Their thermal-hydraulic code captured the
majority of the experimentally observed trends, with the greatest deviation occurring in the
heater-trip transient, where over-predicted heat losses cooled the modeled system consider-
ably faster than was observed experimentally, which they theorize as being resultant from
heat absorbed into the structure of the loop that was not captured in the model. Most
modern experiments similarly use nitrate salts, whose low melting points make them far
easier to work with than the fluoride salts favored for nuclear applications. That rarity
makes the natural circulation FLiBe loop operated at the University of Wisconsin-Madison
by Britsch et al. [22] particularly notable. One finding of theirs deserves emphasis: no
simple correlation between the loop’s temperature readings (∆T , 1/∆T , Tavg , or similar)
and the measured flow velocity could be established, with the best single predictor among
their thermocouples achieving only R2 = 0.56. This complexity is counter-intuitive relative

to the one-dimensional theory of Section 2.1, and it mirrors experience in the present work
(Chapter 6).
     The facility most relevant to the present work is the TAMU natural circulation loop
operated by Reis, Seo, and Hassan [9]. It occupies a unique place in the literature: it
studied water and HITEC salt within an identical geometry, and its transparent quartz
test section allows the use of particle image velocimetry (PIV), providing radially resolved
velocity profiles without perturbing the flow the way a physical flow meter would. Companion
publications document argon injection experiments [23], the consequences of an inadvertent
salt solidification event [24], and the dataset has since served as a validation target for the

[25]. The facility, its instrumentation, and its test matrix are described in detail in Chapter

on the fidelity of the working fluid’s thermophysical properties. Beyond the density variation
that generates the buoyant force itself important properties such as the specific heat deter-

viscosity governs how readily the fluid circulates. Comprehensive single-source property col-

the range of 273.15 K - 1073.15 K and pressures up to 100 MPa [27, 28]. The polyno-

temperature range (Appendix B).

2.3     CFD Simulation of Natural Circulation Loops

Several groups have applied OpenFOAM to natural circulation simulation. Luzzi et al.
[29] validated both a one-dimensional, semi-analytical model and a CFD model made using
OpenFOAM V4 against a rectangular loop that heated its bottom leg and cooled its top
leg. It was found in both the experiments and the corresponding simulations that not
all configurations exhibited stable behavior. This observation of occasional instability is
not an uncommon one for symmetric rectangular and circular geometries. As unstable
configurations tend to be oscillatory, Pini et al. [30] exploited OpenFOAM’s open-source
nature to couple simulation data directly to MATLAB for eigenvalue-based prediction of
oscillatory behavior in rectangular loops. Battistini et al. [31] extended the toolset to
Large Eddy Simulation (LES) of the DYNASTY facility, predicting flow reversals and re-
laminarization, phenomena typical of unstable natural circulation configurations, that had
been absent from previous Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) models of the facility.
   Reviewing these OpenFOAM studies, readers can begin to see how consequential the
choice of how to handle turbulence modeling can be. For modeling natural circulation sys-
tems that are already sensitive to material property inputs, adding the historically tricky
problem of turbulence modeling complicates matters. Nalbandyan et al. [32] demonstrated
this directly, testing three different turbulence models (namely the k − ωSST, k − ωSSTLM,
and realizable k − ϵ models) applied to a 3D CFD against their 1-D model of the DY-
NASTY loop filled with water. Depending on the turbulence model, their relative errors
versus the experiment for mass flow rate varied by 7.2% − 16.2% and their temperature
difference through the cooler section of their loop was off by 1.6K − 2.3K. For molten salt
loops specifically, Simamora and Lee [33] used ANSYS Fluent to compare laminar and tran-
sitional (k-ω SSTLM) treatments of a two-dimensional nitrate salt loop model. The laminar
model underpredicted the circulation velocity by roughly 30%, the transitional model by
only 4.2%. The authors attribute the improvement largely to the improved mixing resultant
from the introduction of turbulence, which allowed the temperature to better penetrate to

                                            15


========== PDF PAGE 30 ==========

the flow’s centerline, allowing the temperature sensitive viscosity to be more representative
of the experiment and thereby better capture the experimental flow profile. This study’s
two-dimensional planar geometry and its reliance on an energy balance, serve as a notable
counterpart to flow features exclusive to three-dimensional modeling that will be discussed
later in Chapter 6.
   For any of these modeling choices to be assessed fairly, discretization error must be con-
trolled, and that control begins with the mesh. Truncation error analysis of the finite-volume
method shows that hexahedral cells produce the smallest truncation errors of the common
cell shapes for a given cell size since they have the smallest possible number of face pairs and
thereby have the least amount of truncation for a given cell count in a given volume, with
tetrahedral cells having the largest error [34]. Hernández-Pérez, Abdulkadir, and Azzopardi
[35] demonstrated that hexahedral topologies likewise produce superior volume-of-fluid (VoF)
results compared to tetrahedral or polar meshes for circular pipe cross-sections. Despite be-
ing out of scopre for this work, the applicability to VoF simulations is important because
the meshing infrastructure built for this thesis is intended to outlive it; extending beyond
single-phase flow to two-phase VoF simulation is planned within the digital twin effort at
the University of Texas at Austin (Chapter 1) and both considerations motivated the purely
hexahedral, structured meshes generated for the present work (Chapter 5).



2.4      Conjugate Heat Transfer and Thermal Boundary

         Conditions

As the flow within an NCL is nothing more than the fluid’s response to its thermal bound-
aries, properly representing those boundaries is of the utmost importance. Fluid-only CFD
simulations often fail to capture the effect that solid materials on a system can have on
the working fluid, especially in transient models with large volumes of material with large
thermal time constants such as many layers of insulation being applied to the external pipe


                                              16


========== PDF PAGE 31 ==========

walls. Capturing these effects generally requires explicit simulation of the solid domains at
the same time, typically referred to as conjugate heat transfer (CHT).
   The many complexities and computational demands of CHT largely disappear, however,
when only the system’s steady state is of interest. For steady-state simulation, the full
CHT approach can be replaced by a mixed (Robin) boundary condition that represents the
wall’s thermal resistances analytically. Ljulj et al. [36] provides a rigorous proof that the
effects of the pipe’s wall are not always negligible and can captured by effective Robin-type
conditions. Their analysis was for a similar case to this work, steady-state cylindrical pipe
flow, but differed by using a single layer and omitting radiation. This work extends the same
construction to potentially thick walls composed of more than one layer and an external
radiation term. This custom boundary condition, rcExternalTemperature is described in
Chapter 5 and verified in Chapter 6.



2.5     Verification and Validation

The credibility of the results in this thesis depend upon the V&V framework applied to it.
Roache [37] is one of the foundational texts for V&V, making the distinction between verifi-
cation, which determines if the intended equations are being solved properly, and validation,
determining if the correct equations are being solved. Oberkampf and Roy [38] extended the
framework into uncertainty quantification and distinguishing between irreducible uncertain-
ties and reducable uncertainties, which must be treated differently and ultimately factor in
to how we determine if the results of this work can be considered validated, and if so, with
what uncertainties attached to it?
   For solution verification, the present work uses the Grid Convergence Index (GCI) proce-
dure set by Celiḱ et al. [12]. The prodcedure is a five-step method that uses Roache’s GCI to
convert a Richardson extrapolation [39] of a set of solutions for a systematically refined mesh
into a standardized uncertainty band. The validity of the GCI is only valid if the solution

lie within the asympototic range of the extrapolated solution and converge monotonically
and the present work was designed to satisfy these conditions, detailed in Chapter 3. For
validation, this work follows ASME V&V 20 [40], the standard for CFD and heat transfer,

2.6     Digital Twins and Reduced-Order Models

The value of high-fidelity CFD simulations extends further beyond direct engineering anal-
ysis. One such application of particular interest in recent years is to use the data generated
by high-fidelity models to inform the creation of reduced-order models (ROMs) or to train
neural networks within digital twin frameworks. As for digital twin application to the nuclear
industry, works such as Kochunas and Huan [10] outline how it is anticipated to improve both
the efficiency and safety of operating nuclear systems. Later works such as Huang et al. [42]
look at the maturation of the technology since Kochunas and Huan and propose a five-tier
hierarchy of maturity for digitial twins in nuclear engineering spanning from guidance and
training from simulation to fully autonomous system operation.
   Almukhametov et al. [43] developed a graph neural network GNN-based digital twin for
a case study to emulate concept reactors such as the Kairos Power FHR with a companion
System Analysis Module (SAM) model manage to achieve an over 100x speed-up compared
to the SAM model while retaining an R2 value of up to 0.995. Hossain et al. [44] demon-
strated DeepONet-based virtual sensors predicting thermal-hydraulic parameters 1,400 times
faster than CFD, with accuracy sufficient for operational monitoring in most cases, but still
experiencing errors of ≈ 10% or more for velocity, turbulent kinetic energy, and pressure
outside of the best case scenario.
   Efforts such as these demonstrate the need for rigorously validated CFD and continued

2.7      Summary and Research Gap

This survey of the literature reveals several key gaps that this thesis addresses:


  1. Limited CFD Validation of Molten Salt Systems: The experimental facility at
      Texas A&M provides the most comprehensive velocity profile dataset for molten salt
      natural circulation loops along with it’s thermocouple readings, yet no independent,
      three-dimensional CFD simulation has been systemically Verified and Validated against
      this data.

  2. Insufficient Boundary Conditions short of full CHT: OpenFOAM’s stock bound-
      ary condition externalTemperature assumes a slab geometry for heat flux through
      surfaces assigned that boundary condition and for cylindrical pipes with thick insula-
      tion, the area ratio between the inner and outer surfaces makes this a poor approxi-
      mation that would otherwise need to be resolved via more complex CHT modeling.

  3. Difficulty with Energy Balance Closure: Previous experiments such as Reis et
      al. have large uncertainties associated with the heater power applied to the system
      not being equal to the calculated rate of heat removal by the system’s cooler, making
      it impossible to accurately model the full system without better representing all the
      heat losses throughout the system.

  4. Accounting for Entry Length Effects: Depending on the geometry of the system
      and the working fluid studied, there can at times be cases where the fluid never fully
      develops in the classical sense for neither its thermal or momentum entry lengths. These
      thermal and momentum entry lengths being fully developed is a typical assumption

3.1      Overview

The V&V process is structured as a sequence of increasingly demanding tests:


  1. Code verification: first, it must be demonstrated that the custom-written code for
      this project, the rcExternalTemperature boundary condition, reproduces an inde-
      pendent analytical reference solution and that the discretization error vanishes at the
      expected rate under mesh refinement.

  2. Solution verification: next, with that verified boundary condition, we need to esti-
      mate the numerical uncertainty of the full-loop simulations for the quantities of interest
      (QoI) by applying Richardson extrapolation and the Grid Convergence Index across a
      family of systematically refined meshes.

  3. Validation: lastly, once the simulations have been verified, we compare how the sim-
      ulation matches up against the experimental measurements of PIV velocity profiles,
      centerline and wall temperatures, and the calculated mass flow rate within the system.


                                              21


========== PDF PAGE 36 ==========

      From there, we judge each comparison against the combined numerical and experimen-
      tal uncertainty.


   This deliberate ordering is because an attempt at validation of the solution is only mean-
ingful once the numerical error is understood and bounded; otherwise, an apparently good
or bad agreement with the experiment cannot be separated from grid-dependence. [37].

methodology to conduct the V&V process.

3.3.1    Convergence Classification

The ratio R = ε21 /ε32 classifies the convergence behavior:

   • 0 < R < 1: Monotonic convergence, where the changes in the solution shrink in
     response to mesh refinement.

   • R < 0: Oscillatory convergence, where the signs of the changes in the solution
     alternate in response to mesh refinement.

   • R > 1: Divergence, where the changes in the solution grow in response to mesh
     refinement

   Only when the solutions converge monotonically is clean Richardson extrapolation using
the GCI estimate permissible. Oscillatory or divergent behavior classifications may signal

                                             24


========== PDF PAGE 39 ==========

that the solutions are not yet within the asymptotic range or that there may be other issues
within the solution and that calculated uncertainty estimates for solution sets with these
behavior classifications should be treated with caution or using specialized methods. This
work’s solutions converge monotonically under mesh refinement so the methods within this
chapter are discussed and later applied with monotonic convergence in mind.

3.4     Boundary Condition Verification

The full-loop’s wall is represented by neither a simple fixed-temperature nor fixed-flux bound-
ary condition, but instead uses a custom boundary condition, rcExternalTemperature,
which solves a one-dimensional, cylindrical conduction problem from the pipe’s inner sur-
face through the composite wall layers to find the external temperature that is then used
to solve for external losses from convective and radiative heat transfer. This was done for
a combination of two reasons: the first was that early on, before the work had been scoped
for a thesis-grade problem, the problem was assumed to be simple and that it would not
need to model the full, solid domain and associated conjugate heat transfer. So, to not
scrap the high-quality fluid domain mesh or significantly retrofit the working pipeline, and
since we are only interested in the steady-state solution, a computed thermal circuit could
prove sufficient. The second reason was, with the first established, OpenFOAM V13 did not
have a stock boundary condition that would properly model the heat transfer for the system
of interest as the closest match, externalTemperature, assumed a slab geometry for it’s
thermal circuit model, which under-predicts losses through the boundary due to the greatly
increased surface area resultant from the thick insulation adorning the facility’s exterior.

4.1      TAMU Natural Circulation Loop

Within the literature of NCL systems, loops with 4 legs are common, and referred to as
"rectangular" on account of these 4-sided systems having a 90-degree rotation between each
leg. The TAMU NCL is special in the sense that has a pair of legs set at an incline rather
than being parallel to the ground in order to encourage a clockwise flow to develop within the
system and avoid some of the instabilities that can result in an inverted flow. This inverted
flow can occur when a rectangular loop is configured with its heater on the bottom leg and a
cooler on its upper leg since the system is symmetric in such a case, the flow can develop in
either a clockwise or counter-clockwise direction [5]. To better differentiate TAMU’s inclined
loop from typical rectangular loops, the facility referenced in this thesis will be referred to
as a "rhombic" loop on account on it’s inclination from parallel and equal-length legs. The
loop operates with two working fluids, water and HITEC molten salt, and uses an identical
pipe configuration for both fluids.


4.1.1     Loop Geometry

The loop’s geometry consists of four legs, each connected at a 90 degree angle with cross
or tee-fittings. Each leg of the loop is nominally 3 feet in length. The top and bottom legs
were bent symmetrically to a nominal incline of 20 degrees with a radius of curvature of
approximately 1 inch, starting roughly 4.325 inches from either end of the pipe. Each pipe
leg had a nominal outer diameter (OD) of 1 inch and a nominal inner diameter (ID) of 0.93
inches and was composed of SS-316 grade stainless steel.
   A brief aside is worth mentioning before continuing with the description of the loop’s
geometry; the reported dimensions are mixed between imperial and metrics units, which is
left as reported by the experimentalists instead of all being converted in an effort to avoid
miscommunications and conflicted reports that arose in the conduction of the work within
this thesis.

Computational Modeling

This chapter describes the two computational models developed for this thesis: a first-order

5.1     First-Order Energy Balance Model

5.1.1    Purpose and Approach

As seen in Chapter 4, there is considerable uncertainty with respect to the global energy
balance of the experimental facility. Lacking information such as detailed insulation config-

prudent to ensure the geometry of the system was well understood as while the publication

model, detailed dimensions, nor Bill of Materials could be provided. Furthermore, the in-

5.1.2     Radial Conduction Solver

For each segment, the radial conduction solver accepts a LoopSegment object defined with
a unique name, segment length, and inner radius, followed by an arbitrary amount of
RadialLayer objects. Those RadialLayer objects are defined by a material, thickness,
and power for segments such as the primary heater on the lower leg and test section that
are electrically heated during operation.
   LoopSegment objects themselves are purely annular models, lacking an internal fluid
volume and associated internal convective heat transfer coefficient. This approach was chosen
for two main reasons, those being: first, to avoid using standard correlations of fully or
partially developed laminar flow while prilimiary calculations showed that the both the
hydrodynamic and thermal entry lengths of both fluids was near or exceeding the total path
length around the loop, in some cases exceeding by several times the loop’s perimeter, as
mentioned in Chapter 2. Secondly, most of the temperature data that was collected, and
especially for typical cylindrical lengths of pipes, was collected using thermocouples on the
external walls of the loop. This combined with the 0.035" pipe walls of stainless steel led to

5.1.4     Composite Fin Model

At each of the corners around the loop, there are structural components that are necessary
to account for in order to close the energy balance of the system. These components are:
three caps for unconnected legs of cross junctions, the drain valve, the melting pot valve,
the bubble injection valve used for other experiments, and the off-gas valve. No temperature
data exsists for any of these regions, so the first order model approximates each of them
as composite, cylindrical fins with a fixed-temperature base. Using this approach, the solid
"fins" away from the system account for between 9-16% of the energy leaving the system.
   The seven cylindrical composite fins were modeled using a two-dimensional axisymmetric
solver:


   • Geometry: Stainless steel core pipe surrounded by insulation sheath of the same
      thickness as the insulation used on the rest of the loop, with an isothermal base held
      at the temperature of the nearest thermocouple reading.

   • Physics: Radial and axial conduction, side-surface natural convection using the same
      cylindrical correlations as above plus radiation, and using horizontal/vertical plate
      correlation for the far-end surface depending on fin orientation.

   • Discretization: Adaptive radial/axial grid (target cell size 0.8 mm, minimum 25 cells
      per direction).

   • Solver: Sparse matrix with linearized radiation, up to 400 iterations, using the same
      tolerances as the radial conduction solver detailed above.


   The total fin heat loss summed over all seven fins is included in the energy balance
closure. The solid-core modeling overestimates the heat transfer for non-solid fins, such as
the off-gas and drain tank valves, but lacking a full-fidelity geometry and additional data in
those regions, this compomise has been made.

5.2.3     Turbulence Modeling

Initially, all the simulations in this work were scoped to only examine laminar modeling of
the experiments as the pipe Reynolds numbers (ReD = 75-1110) are well below the critical
value for turbulent transition (ReD ≈ 2300) in typical pipe flow. However, early simulation
attempts did not recreate the skewed velocity profile shape seen in water tests 3 and 4 and the
flucuations present in the water tests were much greater than the salt test simulations. The
idea that the salt tests were "more laminar" than the water tests led to the idea that some
amount of turbulence was being incurred despite being below the typical critical Reynolds
number. As mentioned in Section 2.1 the long entry lengths delay or prevent full-velocity
development, which would give creedance to shifting the characteristic length of the flow
from the typical dimension of the pipe’s inner diameter, to the height of the loop. Calcu-
lating the Rayleigh number of the system using this characteristic length produces values
exceeding 1010 , which is known as the critical threshold for turbulence in cases involving
natural circulation. Typically in a pipe flow such as this, the transition to turbulence pre-
dicted by a critical Rayleigh number would be suppressed by the full development of the
laminar velocity profile before the transition could occur, but because of our provably long
hydrodynamic entry lengths, we can predict to see some level of turbulence, especially in
water cases 3 and 4; which are exactly the two cases that whose velocity profiles are better
captured by turbulence modeling than laminar modeling as seen in Chapter 6.
   As any turbulence present was theorized to be in the midst of transitioning from laminar
flow, the transitional turbulence model, k − ω SSTLM was chosen as the primary modeling
choice. Secondarily, when researching the k−ω SSTLM model that OpenFOAM uses, it is the
standard formulation that assumes the production of turbulent kinetic energy from buoyant
effects is negligible. So, a codedFvModel was made to reintroduce the buoyant production of

5.3     Mesh Generation

5.3.1    Overview

The computational mesh is generated procedurally using classy_blocks [52], a Python
wrapper around OpenFOAM’s blockMesh utility. The mesh is fully structured hexahedral,
avoiding the numerical diffusion and poor convergence rates associated with tetrahedral or
polyhedral meshes in pipe-flow geometries [34].


5.3.2    Cross-Section Topology

Every pipe segment uses either a 12-block or 20-block O-grid cross-section. The standard
12-block topology is composed of:

   • 4 core blocks form a central square, avoiding the singular axis point of polar meshes.

   • 8 shell blocks form the annular region between the core and the pipe wall.

   The 20-block topology is the same as the 12-block topology with an additional 8 blocks
composing a second annular layer when step transitions are required throughout the loop’s
geometry, as when fitting pipes together.
   This topological approach produces bounded aspect ratios across the entire cross-section
and ensures smooth boundary layer resolution at the wall. The core-to-pipe radius ratio is
derived from the boundary layer grading parameters to ensure a smooth transition from the
graded boundary layer region to the uniform core.

5.3.4    Junction Topology

The four corner junctions use a kernel-cylinder architecture:

   • Kernel: 8 hexahedral Loft blocks forming a near-spherical core, with a kernel_factor
     (default 0.7) controlling the kernel radius as a fraction of the pipe radius and a
     kernel_blend (default 0.5) interpolating between cubic and spherical shapes.

   • Cylindrical legs: One O-grid cylinder per pipe direction extending from the kernel
     to mate with other cylindrical pipe segments.

   • Cap and bridge blocks: Loft blocks connecting kernel faces to cylindrical legs.

   Vertex pairing between the kernel and cylinder legs is resolved by brute-force search
over 16 orientation combinations, with right-hand-rule volume verification to ensure valid
hexahedra.

5.3.5     Bend Segments

The loop contains four pipe bends (two in the lower heater leg, two in the upper cooler leg).
These bends are constructed using Lofts to first connect one face to another and create the
blocks in between before being assigned Arc edges to control the radius of curvature, which
uses a value of 1 inch.


5.3.6     Expanded Cross-Sections

Junction extensions and valve bodies have an expanded outer diameter (12.7 mm radius vs.
the standard 11.811 mm radius). Eight additional concentric ring blocks extend the O-grid
from the bore to the expanded radius, with the annular step face patched as a wall. The
mesh remains fully conformal at these transitions.

5.5      Solver Configuration

5.5.1     Solution Strategy

The simulations use a pseudo-transient approach iteration, using adjustable time-stepping to
stay within the range of stability below a maximum Courant number of 5. This configuration
sacrfices temporal accuracy for computation speed towards steady state, but for an instance
such as this where we are only interested in the steady-state of the simulation, there is no
loss in simulation fidelity. Attempts were made to use a strictly steady-state solver approach,
but were ultimately thrwarted by oscillatory behavior resultant from the sensitive nature of
natural convection and the stagnant initial condition, a better initialized or pseudo-transient-
seeded initial condition may result in stable convergence using a steady-approach, but has not
been tested at this time. These pseudo-transient simulations were run until they sufficiently
converged to steady state. A case was determined to be at steady-state when, over the last

5.5.4    Initial Conditions

The temperature field is initialized uniformly at the mean of the experimental thermocouple
readings for each case. The velocity and pressure fields are initialized to zero. Gravity is
g = (0, −9.81, 0) m/s2 . The simulated loop itself lies within the x-y plane, where views
within this such as Figures 5.1 and 5.2 observe with the z axis oriented towards the camera.

Domain decomposition uses the scotch algorithm. The -copyUniform flag is required during
decomposePar to ensure the custom boundary condition can access uniform field data in
parallel. Simulations were run using the Texas Advanced Computer Center (TACC) and
specifically their LoneStar6 computer cluster, simulations were ran with up to 128 cores.
Scaling performance is reported in Section ??.



5.6     Execution Pipeline

The simulation pipeline is fully automated through the custom foam-vv Python framework,
which orchestrates the following steps for each case:

  1. Case generation: Read YAML configuration files (geometry, physics, study defi-
      nition), create a blockMeshDict via classy_blocks for subsequent mesh generation,
      then rendering OpenFOAM dictionaries from Jinja2 templates.

  2. Mesh generation: Run blockMesh → transformPoints (mm → m) → topoSet (to
      assist with postprocessing objects such as the faceZones for collecting mass flow rate
      data).

  3. Domain decomposition: decomposePar -copyUniform.

  4. Simulation: foamRun in parallel.

6.2      Grid Convergence Study

Regretably, a three-mesh refinement study for the full-loop study could not be completed
across the entire simulation matrix. The full-loop simulation pipeline leveraged the faster
convergence of the coarse mesh to converge to a solution from the initial conditions before
mapping the converged solution from the coarse mesh to the next finer mesh. After mapping
the solution to the medium refinement mesh, the simulation experiences start-up instability
before settling to the new converged state within the refined mesh. The decision ultimately
boiled down to presenting a mix of settled and mostly settled fine-mesh cases with the
full exclusion of the fine-mesh turbulence cases, or the two-refinement study that could
present confident and honest results across the full matrix. The latter option was chosen.
The cost of this includes Richardson extrapolation of the QoIs, discussion of monotonic vs
potential oscillatory convergence, and a larger unum , for each case, but earns a discussion
of the performance of the turbulent models. This is particularly interesting following the
discussion in Section 5.2.3 where we predict to see the turbulent model out-perform the
laminar counterpart on account of the long hydrodynamic entry lengths for water cases 3
and 4.
   Thankfully, Roache provides procedure for cases such as this; where, for performing GCI
on two mesh refinements, the value of Fs used in Equation 3.7 takes the value of 3 instead
of 1.25 [37].

For visually assessing the validation error as it applies to the temperature measurements,
parity plots were chosen. These plot the experimentally recorded value for each temperature
probe within a case; so, for the ideal case of perfectly agreeing predicted and measured
values, the plot’s diagonal line is the target. Along this line, two uncertainty bands are
applied. The thinner, darker band represented only the uncertainty associated with the
instrument recording the data. The broader, lighter line is the applied validation uncertainty
to the target line and if the predicted value falls within that band, it can be classified as

"validated" in the sense the the comparison error cannot be distinguished from other error
sources. The blue bands seen in Figure 6.8 and 6.9 correspond to the four orientation
extremes (front/back top/bottom or left/right) for a given segment of pipe at the nominal
axial point of the wall temperature measurement. With the azimuthal orientations of the
wall thermocouples being lost, the bounds of those four points on the perimeter allow us
to see the effects thermal stratification may have had on the experimental measurements,
which at times is considerable. The temperature parity plots the two turbulence models are
not considerably different from the laminar parity seen in Figure 6.9, so have been excluded
for brevity.
   To pre-empt the next series of plots, it is useful to see the temperature distribution typical
of a molten salt test in the bottom left junction, following just after the heater leg and before
the test section. When looking at the previous parity plots for salt, you may notice that
TP3 is often an outlier with relation to nearly all other measurements and that this trend
is not visible in the water cases. That is because: following the heater leg, the molten salt’s
high Prandtl number results in a steep temperature gradient as the freshly heated, relatively
fast-flowing salt moves up the riser leg, slips past the largely stagnant fluid in the lower
junction. Figure 6.10 shows the steep temperature gradient (specifically for salt test 4 on

6.5      Discrepancies and Possible Explanations

First, the dataset associated with salt test 1 has been determined as faulty in some unknown
way, but with several corresponding reasons. The first aberration within the dataset can be
seen in the Appendix C Table C.1. Specifically, Salt 1’s record of TW10 being greater than
the value recorded for Salt 2; given that these measurements were asserted to be taken at
steady-state, and Salt 2’s power input is greater than Salt 1, it should be impossible to record
a higher temperature baring significantly different environmental effects on account of the
second reason. Second, given the nominal input heater powers, differing by roughly 30-40 W
in each successive salt case, the expected increase in temperature between case 2 and 3 and
then 3 and 4 does not apply between the nominal power increase between salt 1 and salt 2.
Third, for the salt data that was given by the experimentalists, the numbering ran from 2 to
9, citing a failed first test with no usable data among the other unpublished cases. For those
reasons, magnified by the strength of the V&V effort applied to the remaining 7 cases, it
is believed that Salt 1 suffered from data mislabeling, entry error, or other not-understood
misalignment from the presented configuration that was not able to be resolved even with the
help of the experimentalists and has thusly been disqualified from determining the success
of the current work’s Verification and Validation for this dataset.
   Second, despite being technically validated, the large differences in the magnitudes of
Umax for salt test 4 leave room for improvement and on a related note, the difference in
flow development between successive profile heights was much greater for the experimental
case than predicted in this work. This is most likely attributable to the difference in test-
section heating. Reis et al. [24] Figure 8 demonstrates the heat applied to the test section
was concentrated at either end of the quartz glass, whereas the quartz within this model
assumed a uniform distribution of the heat flux. The heat flux being applied through distinct
rings likely resulted in a stronger impulse of buoyant force resultant by the more concentrated
heating at the beginning and end of the test section. The effect of this was potentially further
amplified by the high Prandtl number of molten HITEC not permitting heat to penetrate

to the centerline of the salt, causing a larger gradient in the temperature sensitive viscosity
term of molten HITEC.

Conclusions


7.1      Summary

This thesis presented a comprehensive verification and validation study of three-dimensional
CFD simulations of the TAMU single-phase natural circulation loop, targeting the experi-
mental data of Reis, Seo, and Hassan [9]. The principal contributions are:


  1. An automated CFD modeling pipeline for the TAMU natural circulation loop capable
      of running and validating a model resultant from input configuration files.

  2. An integrated first-order model within the modeling pipeline capable of significantly
      reducing uncertainty associated with the energy accounting of the original experiment.

  3. Successfully verifiied the implementation of the custom rcExternalTemperature bounadry
      condtion as a suitable reduced-order model for cylindrical pipe heat losses when com-
      pared to the effort and computational expense of a full CHT simulation.

  4. Performed systemic analysis to successfully follow the American Society of Mechanical
      Engineer’s standard for Verification and Validation and vaidate the simulation pipeline
      for modeling the experimental effort detailed in Reis et al. [9].



                                              98


========== PDF PAGE 113 ==========

  5. Created a model capable of serving as a suitable starting point for further reduced-order
      models and machine learning training with quantified uncertainty validated within a
      99% confidence interval.



7.2     Future Work

Future work for this project could include any or all of the following aspects:


  1. Extensive turbulence analysis using other RANS models to create a detailed account
      of the modeling choice’s impact on the previously validated simulations and/or ex-
      pansion of the turbulence study to Large Eddy Simulation for higher-resolution of the
      development of turbulence within the system.

  2. Detailed transient analysis beyond the steady-state modeling performed in this work.

  3. Further improvements using reduced-order techniques to further reduce uncertainty
      and improve the quality of the model with potential applicability towards true measure-
      ment predictions rather than simulated predictions performed far after the experiment
      was conducted.

  4. Implementation of the validated model into a Digital Twin framework, potentially
      serving as feedback to inform future experimental design.
